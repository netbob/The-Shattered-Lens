# Act I: The Logic Loop

## Chapter 1: The Ephemeris Error

### Module 1B: The Dakota Avenue Intercept

The air along Dakota Avenue smelled of sulfur and parched dirt, the heavy, alkaline odor of the San Joaquin Valley’s dust bowl cloying the back of Marcus Thorne's throat. It was 05:30 PDT. The sun hadn't broken the horizontal shelf of the Sierra Nevada to the east, but the sky above the peaks had already bled into an unsettling, neon-bright magenta.

Marcus adjusted the mechanical dial on a military-surplus, heavy-cast aluminum antenna tuner bolted to the sidewall of his Ford’s truck bed. His fingers were stiff from the pre-dawn autumn chill. The F-250’s idling engine sputtered periodically, its fuel injectors fighting against an erratic control signal from the onboard computer chassis.

"The clock is slipping on the engine bus," Elena muttered from the open passenger door. She was leaning under the dashboard, a portable oscilloscope clipped to the truck’s diagnostic port. The tiny green tube on her rig displayed a square wave that was visibly flattening at the crown. "The truck’s ECM is dropping frames, Marc. The internal quartz resonator is running hot. If the CAN bus loses its timing window, the fuel pump logic will latch shut. We’ll be stranded on the shoulder with forty gallons of unburned diesel in the tanks."

"It's not just the truck," Marcus said. He didn't look down at her. He kept his eyes locked on the custom five-inch cathode-ray marine radar display resting in the green magnesium field desk on his lap. "The entire regional packet network is stuttering. Every switch between here and Bakersfield is dropping its routing tables because the core routers can't verify their hardware signatures."

He reached out, shifting a heavy, double-pole toggle switch that cut the truck’s battery output directly to his custom shortwave assembly. The radio was an exercise in industrial archaeology: an old, military-contract Collins receiver from the late 1960s, stripped of its original tubes and retrofitted with high-vacuum ceramic triodes salvaged from a decommissioned radar installation at the old Castle Air Force Base. No microprocessors. No solid-state logic gates. Just copper inductors, silver-plated ceramic wafers, and brute-force current amplification.

Beside the radio sat an old mechanical teleprinter—a Teletype Model 32, its yellowed paper roll fed through an oil-stained steel carriage. It was wired into the Collins receiver via a low-frequency terminal node controller that converted radio frequency shifts into physical, high-voltage current pulses.

A sudden, sharp screech whistled through the Collins' external speaker—a raw, unmodulated binary frequency-shift keying (FSK) tone slicing through the background static of the 40-meter band.

The Teletype’s internal motor shrieked, its heavy metal typing head striking the paper with the rhythmic, concussive sound of a machine gun firing in an enclosed room.

KH6HAK > W6TRN [ARQ PACKET CONNECTED]
RAW TELEMETRY INVERSION BURIED FROM SITE 2
AUTHENTICATION INVALIDATED BY REMOTE CORE
RECORD THIS MANUALLY MARCUS

Marcus grabbed a graphite pencil from the truck's dashboard, his hand dropping to the margins of the yellowed paper roll as the mechanical keys hammered down. "It's Aris," he yelled over the mechanical clatter of the machine. "He's tunneling out of Haleakala using the old Hawaiian inter-island emergency ionospheric bounce. The main fiber cables under the Pacific must be dropping their data packets."

The Teletype shattered into a frantic tempo, its ribbon carriage jumping spaces before spitting out the raw astrometric telemetry confirmed by the Atacama data bridge:

OBJ: A-9 // TIDALLY FRAGMENTED COMET CORE // COLLAPSED ALBEDO
RA: 22 14 08.23 // DEC: +12 04 11.1 // APERTURE REF: 1.8M
VECTOR: TRAJECTORY INVERSION CONFIRMED BY CHILE APER NODE
PRIMARY MASS INTERSECT POINT CONCEPTION SHELF // SEC-FRAG ENTRY INSTANTANEOUS
IMPACT WINDOW: < 72 HOURS
WARN: CENTRAL DATA PLATFORMS ARE PURGING FILES TO MAINTAIN FALSE CONTEXT

"Point Conception?" Elena scrambled out from under the dashboard, her boots crunching on the gravel shoulder. She leaned over Marcus’s shoulder, staring at the purple ink blooming on the paper roll. "That’s three hundred miles west. Marc, if an eleven-kilometer rock hits the shallow shelf at forty kilometers a second, the displacement wave will breach the coastal range. The hydraulic back-surge will clear the whole valley floor up to the five-hundred-foot line."

"The water isn't what kills us first," Marcus said, his voice dropping as the Teletype suddenly ceased its rhythmic thumping. The line tone dropped into an unmodulated, hollow hiss.

On his lap, the five-inch green phosphor screen on the marine radar display suddenly bloomed into a brilliant, circular ring of absolute white light. The glass tube crackled—a distinct, high-voltage ping echoing from the copper neck assembly inside the casing. A trace ribbon of white smoke, smelling of scorched resin and selenium rectifiers, leaked through the ventilation louvers of the field desk.

The truck's engine died instantly, the constant rumble of the diesel cylinders dropping into an abrupt, dead silence. The lights on the dashboard didn't dim; they didn't flicker. The digital odometer display simply went gray, its liquid crystal substrate turning opaque as the underlying driving circuits locked in a permanent, high-voltage short.

"The ionization wavefront," Marcus whispered. He set the dead field desk on the truck's tailgate and stood up, looking eastward across the vast, flat expanse of the agricultural grids.

The advanced, trailing fragments of the comet array had struck the upper ionosphere over the eastern Pacific, shearing the atomic bonds of the stratosphere. The air above the mountains was no longer dark. The magenta glow along the ridge line of the Sierra Nevada had vanished, replaced by an expanding, silent wall of deep, violet light that seemed to rise directly out of the mountain stone. It didn't radiate from a source; it was as if the atmosphere itself had become an energized gas tube, glowing with an intense, unblinking luminescence that cast no shadows.

Marcus checked his wrist. His modern digital field watch was dead, its screen a smooth slate of black glass.

He reached into his vest pocket and pulled out his grandfather’s mechanical artillery stopwatch. He pressed the top plunger with his thumb. The long, sweeping steel hand began to tick—a crisp, mechanical click-click-click that cut through the silent, violet dawn on Dakota Avenue.

He tore the yellowed paper telemetry sheet from the Teletype’s carriage roller with a sharp crack. He opened his massive leather accounting ledger across the closed tailgate of the dead truck, aligning the torn paper edge against the heavy linen margin of Page One.

"Seventy-two hours," Marcus said, dipping his steel nib straight into the well without checking a screen. His hand was deliberate as he made the very first manual entry into the ledger of the new world. "The silicon is dead, El. The networks just turned into sand. Get the manual siphon lines out of the back. We need every gallon of fuel from that storage tank before the air turns into brick."
