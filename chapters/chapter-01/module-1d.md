# Act I: The Logic Loop

## Chapter 1: The Ephemeris Error

### Module 1D: The Synchronization Horizon

The transition from a digital society to a silent landscape did not occur with a sudden explosive roar, but with a series of quiet, systemic subtractions.

By 06:10 AM PDT on Sunday, October 25, 2026, the synchronization horizon across North America had completely collapsed.

In the high-density routing facilities of the Pacific Northwest, automated optical switches began dropping their wavelength-division multiplexing arrays because they could no longer verify the integrity of the border gateway protocol handshakes. The lines didn't melt; they simply stopped passing bits, leaving the transoceanic fiber pipelines humming with dead carrier waves that terminated in empty glass terminals.

Down on Dakota Avenue, the silence was total.

Marcus Thorne stood on the gravel shoulder, his grandfather’s mechanical stopwatch ticking against his ribs with a dry, metallic cadence. He wasn't watching the road anymore. He was watching the sky over the Central Valley, where the violet ionization glow had begun to form a vast, shimmering lens that warped the cold starlight of the dawn.

Beside him, Elena had finished rigging the manual brass fuel pump to the truck's auxiliary transfer tank. Her knuckles were raw, stained with grease and the cold morning soot. She didn't use her phone to calculate the volume; she used a notched wooden dipstick, dropping it into the dark fluid and reading the wet line against the carved marks.

"Forty-two gallons," she said, her voice dropping into the quiet of the fields. "That’s enough to get us to the Pine Ridge foothills if we use the old logging tracks, Marc. But if the highways are choked with people looking for a signal, we won't make five miles before we have to abandon the rig."

"They won't be looking for a signal on the logging tracks," Marcus said, his pencil poised over the heavy paper of the county ledger. "They’ll be waiting in the suburbs, staring at their dashboards, waiting for the navigation screens to tell them which way is north. In forty-eight hours, when the water system valves lock up because their local logic controllers timed out, they’ll start moving on foot. We need to be past the foothill gate before they realize the paper is the only thing that stays valid."

He didn't look back at the dead Ford. He closed the leather-bound book, tucked the inkwell into his canvas vest, and pointed his boots toward the rising wall of the Sierra Nevada. The world had officially split into two distinct factions: those who were waiting for the lights to come back on, and those who were already writing down the names of the survivors with graphite and iron.
