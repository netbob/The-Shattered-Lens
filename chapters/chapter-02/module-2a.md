# Module 2A: The Grid-Lock of Clovis

The automated municipal water distribution node for the City of Clovis, located near the intersection of Herndon and Fowler Avenues, did not suffer a physical pipe failure or a pressure valve blowout. It died of a silent chronological calculation.

At exactly 10:40 AM, the local Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) terminal—a hardened industrial PC running an un-networked real-time operating system—attempted to execute its automated bi-weekly chlorine injection sequence. To log the chemical volume safely into the municipal archive, the machine's local security policy required a valid cryptographic handshake with the valley’s regional infrastructure directory.

The console log on the utility panel flickered three times in the dim concrete vault:

```text
[WARN]  [10:40:02Z] scada_pump_04: Verification epoch closed. Upstream clock invalid.
[FATAL] [10:40:05Z] scada_integrity: Cryptographic certificate 'CLOVIS-MUNI-NET' has expired.
[SYSTEM] [10:40:06Z] Safety protocol 90-A invoked. Locking injection ports. System state: LATCHED.
```

The heavy, electric-drive main pumps hummed down, their variable-frequency drives dropping from sixty hertz to a dead, vibrating stop. Across five square miles of suburban tracts, from the manicured lawns of Loma Vista to the clean concrete curbs of the new subdivisions, the water pressure in the copper lines began to fall, dropping from sixty pounds per square inch to a hollow, sputtering trickle.

Three miles away, sitting on an upturned plastic crate in the shade of a dead cellular tower near Dakota Avenue, Marcus Thorne was sharpening his graphite pencil with a pocket knife.

The yellowed Teletype sheet from the Haleakala intercept was pasted cleanly across the linen surface of Page One of his heavy leather ledger. He didn't look toward the city. He was focused on the clean copperplate letters he was drawing into the column headings on Page Two.

"The line pressure just died in the irrigation lateral behind the canal," Elena said. She walked back from the ditch, carrying her notched wooden dipstick. The tip was dry. "The municipal pumps must have tripped their logic safety gates. The whole grid is going dry, Marc. People in town are going to open their taps, see nothing but air, and start looking at their phones for an emergency alert."

"They won't find one," Marcus said without looking up, his carbon tip leaving a sharp, black line across the page. "The cellular transponders require a verified network signature every four hours to maintain their spectrum licenses. By noon, the towers will drop their carrier waves entirely to prevent unauthenticated interference loops. They’ll be trapped in their own living rooms, staring at black glass, trying to reboot their phones until the batteries drain into sand."

He turned the page, the heavy cotton paper making a distinct, crisp snap in the stagnant, silent air of the valley.

"Let them stare," Marcus muttered, his eyes tracking down the empty columns where the names of the survivors would soon have to be recorded. "We have twenty-four hours before they realize that the only water left in this basin is what’s sitting in the bottom of the old manual farm wells. Write down the tool inventory, El. Every shovel, every bucket, every foot of hemp rope. If it doesn't need an administrative key to function, it gets logged on Page Three."
