# Module 2E: The Foothill Ridge Gate

The physical barrier at the Foothill Ridge Gate—the final vehicle access control point blocking the logging grades into the high country of Pine Ridge—was an automated, high-tensile steel boom arm counterweighted with a five-hundred-pound concrete block. It did not drop due to a broken cable; it sat frozen at a horizontal thirty-degree angle, its heavy hydraulic locking pin driven home into the steel sleeve when the remote telemetry node on the ridge lost its carrier signal from the valley.
Marcus Thorne let the straps of his heavy leather ledger case drop from his shoulders, allowing the massive book to rest across the top plate of the handcart. The gray volcanic silt was now falling in a steady, silent drift, dusting the shoulders of his canvas coat with a dry, salt-like powder that smelled intensely of burnt sulfur and pulverized slate.
Behind him, Elena lowered the handles of the handcart. Her palms were raw, the canvas work gloves she wore worn through to the skin at the thumbs from three hours of fighting the wheel tracks up the steep granite incline.
"The hydraulic circuit has an internal battery back-up, Marc," she said, her voice dropping into the quiet of the pines. She reached down, using her pocket knife to clear a crust of ash from the access panel of the gate's mechanical housing. "The accumulator valve is an electronic solenoid. It needs a twelve-volt pulse to drop the pin and let the counterweight swing the arm up. The manual override lever is behind a magnetic deadbolt that won't release unless the internal logic board sees a valid supervisor badge signature."
Marcus didn't touch the iron housing. He unbuckled the brass clasps of the ledger case, opening the heavy cover to Page Three. The yellowed paper insert from the Haleakala intercept sat perfectly aligned on Page One, its purple ink text clean and un-smeared under its protective sheet of clear oilcloth.
"The badge signatures are dead, El," Marcus said. He picked up his mechanical pencil, clicking the lead out three times until the graphite tip was sharp enough to trace the lines of his tool index. "The database that checks the supervisor codes was wiped out forty miles ago when the central switching station in Fresno entered its validation loop timeout. If we wait for the logic to clear, the mud from the delta back-surge will catch us before we hit the four-hundred-foot line. We don't ask the machine for a signature. We use the bypass rules."
He reached into the handcart, bypassing the rations and the water tins, his fingers closing around the cold iron shaft of a five-foot railroad pry-bar.
"Hold the book," Marcus muttered, slipping the wedge end of the bar directly under the seam where the steel casing met the concrete foundation block.
Elena stepped up to the cart, her hands pressing flat against the margins of the paper to shield the open columns from the falling soot. "If we break the pin casing, the automated alarms will latch the secondary gates three miles up the grade, Marc. The system is designed to isolate the entire watershed if it tracks a physical breach at the perimeter fence."
"The system doesn't have a network to pass the alarm to," Marcus said, his teeth grinding together as he threw his full weight against the iron bar.
The concrete beneath the casing cracked—a sharp, distinct snap of fracturing aggregate that echoed through the dark trees like a pistol shot. The hydraulic cylinder sheared at its mounting pin, its pressurized oil spraying a dark, greasy line across the gray silt of the road before the massive steel arm swung violently upward, its counterweight plunging into the dirt with a heavy, hollow thud.
The road into Pine Ridge sat open, a steep, unpaved gray ribbon climbing through the dark timber toward a sky that was now a solid, unblinking lens of violet light.
Marcus dropped the pry-bar into the bottom of the handcart. He didn't look back toward the valley floor, where the distant, geometric lights of the Clovis suburbs had completely vanished beneath a rolling wall of black soot and sea fog. He picked up his pen, dipped the steel nib into his inkwell, and drew a single, heavy horizontal stroke across the bottom of Page Three, closing out the inventory of the old world.
"Entry one," Marcus wrote, his copperplate script clean and immutable against the cold paper. "The Foothill Ridge Gate. Overridden by physical leverage this twenty-fifth day of October. The path is verified."
