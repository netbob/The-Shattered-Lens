# Module 3D: The Registry Line

The approach to the Foothill Ridge Gate was no longer an unpaved logging track; it had become an bottleneck of human exhaustion.
By 3:00 PM on October 26, the vanguard of the migration from the valley floor had hit the eight-hundred-foot elevation line. They did not arrive in vehicles. The asphalt of Herndon and Fowler Avenues had become a permanent gridlock of dead internal combustion blocks, their solid-state engine control modules shorted into slag by the ionospheric wavefront. They arrived on foot, their clothes stiff with the gray, cement-like mud of the delta back-surge, their faces wrapped in torn rags to filter out the superheated sulfur air.
Marcus Thorne stood behind the heavy timber gatepost of the shattered boom arm, his grandfather’s mechanical stopwatch ticking with an uninterrupted, dry cadence in his waistcoat pocket. On the flat timber surface of a salvaged three-plank picnic table sat the central ledger, its heavy leather cover pinned open by two iron wedges.
Elena Vance stood ten paces to his left, her canvas work gloves discarded, her bare right hand resting on the smooth walnut stock of her Winchester rifle. Her boots were caked in three inches of the falling volcanic slurry, her eyes permanently squinted against the strange, violet luminescence pulsating through the heavy mountain fog.
"You can't close the road," a man at the front of the line screamed. His voice was raspy, his throat parched by the alkaline dust. He was wearing an expensive, oil-stained high-tech windbreaker, his left hand clutching a dead iPhone like a protective talisman. "The county signs down at the highway say this is public watershed land. My family has three days of water left in our packs. We have a right to the reservoir access."
Marcus did not look up from the open page. He clicked his mechanical pencil three times, the lead tip settling into the column line of Page Four.
"The county network dropped its administrative licenses at dawn," Marcus said, his voice carrying a flat, rhythmic weight that cut through the low groans of the crowd behind the gate. "The public land titles vanished when the central servers in Fresno entered their validation loop timeout. There is no public watershed. There is only the Registry."
He dipped his head toward the table, the carbon tip drawing a clean, un-smeared line under the last verified entry.
"Your name," Marcus said.
The man stepped forward, his thumb automatically hammering the side button of his dead handset, expecting a screen to bloom into life. "Robert Hayes," he muttered, his eyes dropping to the blank glass. "I’m an infrastructure supervisor for the irrigation district. My badge code is—"
"We don't use badge codes," Marcus interrupted, his pencil tip hovering over the paper. "The database that reads your code is currently buried under forty feet of saltwater in the delta sloughs. If you want to cross into the high country, your digital identity stays at the gate. Step to the scrap pile."
Elena pointed a silent, wrapped finger toward a five-foot-wide heap of discarded electronics growing on the gravel shoulder. It was a mound of black glass, aluminum casings, and dead lithium batteries—hundreds of thousands of dollars of consumer technology treated as raw, un-magnetized tool iron.
"Drop the handset, Mr. Hayes," Elena said, her voice dropping an octave as she shifted the rifle barrel by a fractional inch. "And the car keys. Anything with a silicon wafer goes into the iron stack. Then you step back to the desk, you sign your physical mark in the ledger, and you accept your caloric credit. If you keep the glass, you turn around and walk back down to the water line."
Hayes looked back down the grade. Behind him, stretching for two miles through the dark ponderosa pines, thousands of shapes were moving through the gray gloom—a silent, desperate column of refugees fleeing a valley floor that was rapidly transforming into an land-locked ocean of gray mud.
He didn't speak. He dropped his arm, letting the iPhone fall onto the pile with a sharp, hollow clack against the chassis of a dead engine control module.
Marcus wrote the name in his clean, nineteen-century copperplate script: Robert Hayes. One soul. Former status: null. Current credit: two units. [1]
He picked up a small, hand-stamped lead token from a wooden tray and slid it across the raw pine grain of the table. "Move three hundred yards up the grade to the secondary silo," Marcus said. "Surrender the token to the gatekeeper for four ounces of dry salt pork and a pint of well water. Your credit is logged. You belong to the paper now."
A low-frequency rumble shuddered through the granite beneath their boots—the acoustic boom of another trailing comet fragment shearing through the stratosphere over the eastern peaks. The empty pages of the ledger caught the violet shadow of the sky, but the graphite lines stayed clean, black, and completely fixed in the stone.
