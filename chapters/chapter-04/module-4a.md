# Module 4A: The Frost Floor

The transition into the subterranean map room of the Pine Ridge Strategic Air Command facility did not resemble an entry into a modern bunker. It was a regression into a tomb of unpainted concrete and zinc-coated conduit that had been abandoned by the federal government when the first solid-state defense networks were routed through the silicon valleys in 1974.
The air inside Pod 1 was dry, dead, and precisely thirty-eight degrees Fahrenheit, cooled by forty feet of solid granite mountain overhead. The only light came from three low-voltage, kerosene-burning trench lanterns hung from the overhead pipe racks, their flickering yellow flames casting long, smoky shadows across the eighty-two survivors packed along the concrete floor.
Marcus Thorne sat at the central desk—an old steel field unit propped up on two crates of legacy military-surplus ration tins. The locked leather ledger case sat in the center of the iron top plate, its brass padlock catching the greasy amber reflection of the lantern wicks.
Beside him, Elena was using an analog wire-gauge wheel to measure the thickness of a spool of salvaged copper telephone wire they had stripped from the facility's internal communication conduits.
"The temperature outside the blast door dropped eighteen degrees in forty minutes," Elena said, her voice dropping to a whisper to avoid waking the rows of children huddled under wool blankets along the east wall. "The silica dust in the upper atmosphere is reflecting the solar continuum cleanly back into space. The trees are splitting on the ridge, Marc. The sap inside the ponderosas is freezing solid enough to pop the bark right off the trunks."
"The impact winter has arrived forty-eight hours ahead of Aris’s models," Marcus said. He didn't unclamp the ledger case. He picked up a piece of white chalk and drew a clean grid matrix across the surface of a split slate panel resting on his knee. "The troposphere is locking down. Without an automated heating grid or a diesel boiler to cycle steam through the floorboards, our caloric burn rate is going to double before the first week of November ends."
He looked down the rows of human shapes sleeping on the bare concrete. They were safe from the water and the immediate toxic sulfur mix of the valley, but they were trapped inside a cold-chassis vault that possessed no digital switches, no automated climate zones, and no connection to an external world that could push an emergency license update.
"Let the mountain freeze," Marcus muttered, his chalk scratching a sharp, geometric 'I' into the primary column of the allocation tally. "The concrete doesn't care about the frost floor, and the ledger doesn't require an external power loop to maintain its integrity. We count the calories manually every twelve hours. If we keep the ledger locked and the ink dry, the frost won't be able to erase who we are."
