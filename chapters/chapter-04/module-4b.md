# Module 4B: The Lithic Thermocline

The thermal drop across the face of the Book Cliffs did not follow the standard adiabatic lapse rates of the high desert plateau. By October 29, the atmospheric inversion layer—now a solid, opaque stratospheric blanket of volatile formaldehyde haze and micro-crystalline sea salt—had throttled the solar input to less than three percent of baseline irradiance.
The rock did not cool gradually; it shed its stored summer heat in a rapid, continuous infrared bleeding that caused the ancient, iron-stained Mancos shale layers to expand and crack with the sound of breaking slate.
David Sterling crawled into a narrow structural fissure three hundred feet above the desert floor, his breath freezing instantly into a fine, gray rime across the front of his canvas waistcoat. The temperature inside the canyon rift had dropped to twelve degrees below zero, driven by a persistent, high-pressure katabatic wind that swept down from the Tavaputs Plateau like a spillway of liquid nitrogen.
His fingers, wrapped in strips of coarse electrical tape salvaged from the dead diagnostic rigs of Pod 4, had lost all secondary tactile sensitivity. He could no longer feel the individual knurled clicks on the brass casing of his mechanical pocket watch, but he could feel the steady, sub-audible vibration of its escape wheel ticking against his ribs.
He unbuckled his pack with his teeth, letting the heavy canvas straps drop into the sand. He reached inside, bypassing his frozen rations, and pulled out the rectangular slab of laser-milled tool steel.
The plate was no longer just a structural index of a dead cryptographic directory; it had become an immediate thermal hazard. At twelve below zero, the un-magnetized iron was cold enough to tear the skin from his palm upon direct contact.
David didn't drop it. He wrapped the metal template in an oilcloth sleeve, using his body weight to wedge the sharp edge of the plate directly into a horizontal bedding plane of the sandstone rift. It served as a makeshift windbreak—a single, unyielding seven-pound iron wedge shielding the tiny flame of his manual flint wheel from the pressurized draft of the canyon.
"The servers didn't freeze," he whispered into the black throat of the rock gap, his teeth clicking together so hard the words were sheared into fragments. "The data frames are intact. They’re sitting on the magnetic platters inside the vaults, perfectly preserved in twenty-year-old cold storage, waiting for a clock signature that doesn't exist anymore."
He leaned his forehead against the cold sandstone.
Through the narrow opening of the fissure, the desert below looked like a vast, featureless basin of gray salt. The sky over the Green River valley was a deep, unblinking monochrome indigo, lit only by the pale, fluorescent shimmer of the stratospheric lens. There were no lights to the west. The regional infrastructure networks of the Great Basin had entered their final, permanent state of terminal zero trust, leaving nothing but a wilderness of frozen stone where a man's identity was defined strictly by the weight of the iron he could carry on his back.
