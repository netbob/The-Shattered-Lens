# Module 4C: The Gravity Escape

The governor flyweights on the mechanical clockwork drive at the Pan-STARRS 2 dome did not slow to a controlled crawl due to friction variance. By October 30, the mechanical tracking weights—three hundred pounds of salvaged industrial counterweight iron suspended by aircraft cable inside the telescope’s concrete structural pier—had frozen directly to the internal drainage shaft wall.
The high-altitude frost hummed through the concrete deck, the ambient summit temperature plunging to twenty-four below zero as the stratospheric formaldehyde lens choked off the last vestige of the solar thermal baseline.
Aris Thorne stood on the sub-zero iron scaffolding of the Cassegrain focus cage, his breath catching in thick, white ice needles against the brass rim of his visual eyepiece. He was no longer using his gloves; they had frozen stiff after absorbing a spill of un-cycled hydraulic fluid, leaving his fingers wrapped in strips of rough canvas backing to protect his skin from the bare metal.
"The tracking drive is failing, June," Aris called out, his voice sharp and rattling against the dark sheet-iron panels of the closed dome shutter. "The gear train is slipping through the ice shelf inside the pier. The stellar field is drifting out of the transit window westward at fifteen arcseconds per minute. If we lose the tracking vector completely, I can't calculate the secondary orbital decay paths for the California ridge link."
Below the telescope fork, June Miller was standing inside the concrete pier shaft, a five-foot steel crowbar held between her raw palms. The only light came from the dull tallow flame of a salvaged emergency lantern, its wick sputtering in the oxygen-depleted, frozen air of the lower vault.
"The condensation from the cooling lines has frozen into an unyielding three-inch ice shelf inside the guide pipe," June yelled up, her voice metallic and hollow against the iron casing. "The counterweights are locked solid. The gravity drive doesn't have the torque to shear through the ice layer."
"Disconnect the governor linkage," Aris shouted back, his hand locking onto the visual micrometer screw of his hand-held spectrograph. "Drop the gear train into direct manual slip. We’ll counter-weight the primary fork using our own bodies if the cable stays clear."
June didn't answer with words. A heavy, resounding clonk of iron hitting frozen aggregate echoed through the deck as she slammed the crowbar against the primary release latch of the gear train.
The mechanical governor assembly spun loose with a high-pitched, screaming whine of un-lubricated brass cogs turning without a governor brake.
The entire mass of the 1.8-meter primary aperture telescope lurked on its bearing axis, its massive steel yoke dropping hard toward the western horizon as gravity took the un-braked load.
Aris scrambled along the observer's cradle, his arm throwing its weight across the top rail of the instrument ring to serve as a living friction brake. His boots skidded across the frosted steel, his muscles tearing against the sheer momentum of the swinging reflector before the mechanical safety block caught the fork with a dull, echoing shudder.
He didn't check his arms for bruising. He instantly slammed his face back against the visual lens, his right hand catching the rough lead tip of his mechanical pencil to note the coordinates before the frost could fog the optical glass.
Through the 1.8-meter eye, the Pegasus sector was no longer an abstraction of digital sky tracking. The star field had stabilized, held in place by the physical deadweight of two human bodies anchoring an old iron frame against the mountain’s frost.
