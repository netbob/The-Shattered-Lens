# Module 4D: The Caloric Tally

The reduction of the baseline ration inside Pod 1 did not provoke an immediate vocal outcry from the families crowded along the concrete floor grids. It was received with the flat, unblinking silence of a population whose sensory limits had already been exhausted by the three-day descent of the impact winter.
By November 1, 2026, the interior temperature of the Strategic Air Command map room had stabilized at thirty-four degrees Fahrenheit—exactly two degrees above the freezing point of the auxiliary water accumulator tins stacked against the bulkhead. The air had grown thick, smelling of stale sweat, damp wool, and the oily soot of the three kerosene trench lanterns that served as the vault's only illumination.
Marcus Thorne sat behind the iron top plate of his field desk, a piece of dry calcium chalk held between his cracked, ink-stained fingers. On his lap sat the split slate panel, its dark surface cross-hatched with sixty-four clean geometric columns tracking the remaining bulk stores in the secondary silo.
He did not unlock the primary ledger case. The heavy leather book remained buckled inside its zinc brackets, its brass padlock cold to the touch. The ledger was the immutable record of their names; the slate was for the rolling math of their survival.
"The inventory from the lower surface shaft is short four cases of salt pork, Marc," Elena said. She stepped up to the desk, her boots caked in a dry, white rime of frost that she had scraped from the interior seal of the blast door. Her hands were tucked deep into her coat sleeves, her face drawn and yellowed under the amber lantern light. "The dampness in the ventilation tunnel breached the wax seals on the crate frames. The brine leaked out. Half the jars have split from the frost floor pressure."
Marcus did not clear his columns. He drew a heavy, single white stroke through the integer 4.0 in the third tier of his matrix, reducing the daily allocation code from twelve ounces to eight per registered adult name.
"That drops the net caloric buffer to fourteen hundred units per day," Marcus said, his voice flat and mechanical in the dead air of the pod. "At thirty-four degrees, the basal metabolic rate requires sixteen hundred just to prevent tissue deterioration in the children's ward. If the soot layer doesn't thin by the middle of the month, we’ll be burning through our seed grain before the first wood-cutting crew can clear the timber ice outside."
A man rose from a cluster of blankets twenty feet down the row—Robert Cross, his fingers still wrapped in the dirty mechanic's rags he had worn since surrendering his handset at the Foothill Ridge Gate. His breath rose in a faint, rapid cloud that vanished instantly into the dark overhead pipe racks.
"You can't cut the salt pork line, Thorne," Cross said, his voice raspy from the sulfur dust that still lingered in his lungs. "My crew spent ten hours on the manual crank pump today just to keep the deep well from freezing solid. We can't clear the intake valves on four ounces of meat and a pint of well water. The iron handles are cold enough to split a man's palm open if he doesn't have grease on his gloves."
Marcus looked up from the slate. He didn't check his watch; he felt the rhythm of the ticking universal chronometer inside his waistcoat vest.
"The allocation is pinned to the physical volume inside the vault, Mr. Cross," Marcus said, his eyes tracking the man's face with the absolute neutrality of an auditor. "If I write a larger number on this slate, the pork doesn't multiply in the silo. It just means the book goes blank four weeks early. If your crew drops their shift on the pump, the water freezes, the Registry line terminates, and the names don't exist past Tuesday. The math doesn't answer to your hunger."
Cross didn't advance toward the desk. He looked at Elena's bare right hand resting on the smooth stock of her Winchester rifle, then down at the heavy brass lock sealing the leather case. The silence of the eighty-two survivors in Pod 1 closed back over the room like ice sealing a pond, leaving nothing but the steady, rhythmic click-click-click of Marcus’s stopwatch keeping time for a world that had nothing left but its names and the weight of its paper.
