# Module 4E: The Baseline Settlement

The processing of the first internal death inside Pod 1 did not wait for a county coroner’s certificate or a digitized vital-statistics file. The state’s central database in Sacramento had completed its structural self-termination four days prior, its automated cryptographic verification loops permanently sealing the registry servers against their own administrators.
Arthur Miller, a sixty-eight-year-old retired diesel mechanic from the Clovis irrigation pool, died at 04:12 AM on November 2, 2026. The cause was not kinetic trauma from the Astraea-9 impact fragments, but the rapid accumulation of fine, sub-micron silica dust in his lower bronchial tracts—the gray, cement-like ash that had bypassed the coarse wool filtration rags he had worn during the migration up the Fowler canal [3D].

Marcus sat behind the iron top plate, his leather ledger case unclasped but held down by his left forearm [4A, 4D]. He did not use his chalk or his temporary slate panel for this entry [4D]. He had cleared the oiled parchment from Page Seven of Book I, his steel nib dipped deep into the viscous, soot-and-vinegar inkwell.
"The ground outside the secondary blast door is frozen down to eighteen inches," Elena said. The hickory handles are splitting from the cold before the iron teeth can bite into the granite wash."
"We don't use the ridge," Marcus said, his voice flat, carrying the mechanical rhythm of the universal chronometer ticking inside his vest pocket [4D]. "We use the dead-storage chamber in the third surface shaft. The space is unheated, the frost floor is uniform, and it’s isolated from the well intake lines. Seal the body in an oilcloth wrap and number the canvas with a grease crayon."
He lowered his hand to the paper, the steel nib scratching a clean, nineteen-century copperplate line into the first empty column of the mortality register.

"You’re striking his name before we’ve even moved him," a woman whispered from the dark rows of blankets—Sarah Miller, her face gray, her hands shaking as she held a dead Android tablet like a block of un-carved wood. Her voice was thin, rattling against the low concrete ceiling of the pod. "He spent thirty years servicing the municipal pumps for this county. He has a pension record on the state utility network. You can't just cross him out with a pencil like a broken tool."
Marcus did not look up from the ink. He drew a clean, single horizontal rule through the integer credit score next to Arthur Miller's name, then slid a small, hand-stamped lead token into a wooden drawer beneath the desk plate [3D, 4D].
"The state utility network is sand, Mrs. Miller," Marcus said, his eyes tracking the wet ink as it dried into the grain of the rag paper. "The pension records vanished when the master cryptographic authorities locked their vaults to protect their own integrity licenses [1C]. The Registry does not calculate historical status or vanished promises. We calculate the physical weight of the salt pork sitting in our silo and the exact volume of water our crews can draw through the ice by hand [4D]."
He looked across the room at the eighty-two survivors huddled beneath the amber glow of the lanterns [4A, 4D]. None of them spoke. They watched his pen move with the quiet, survival-driven focus of an audience watching a machine log their own expiration dates.
"The allocation for entry six-hundred-and-fifteen is closed," Marcus muttered, his fingers tightening around the brass handle of his ledger case as he swung the leather cover shut, locking the brass padlock with a sharp, final clank [3D, 3E, 4D]. "The calories stay with the living names. We count the balance again at dusk."
