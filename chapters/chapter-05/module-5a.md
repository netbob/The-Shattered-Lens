# Module 5A: The Iron Fracture

The primary blast door of Pod 1 did not break its seal with a hydraulic hum. The four heavy locking dogs—machined from solid zinc-plated carbon steel in 1968—were jammed fast within their wall sleeves, held in place by an eighteen-inch external collar of frozen volcanic slurry that had leaked through the emergency intake vents during the midnight storm.
Elena Vance stood on the lower step of the gantry, a twelve-pound sledgehammer swung over her right shoulder [4A]. Her face was wrapped in thick layers of canvas backing, her skin showing a raw, red wind-scorch where the cloth parted at the cheekbone [4C, 4E].
"The seal is cross-threaded from the overpressure front, Marc," she said, her voice muffled by the rags. She shifted her boots against the concrete riser, trying to find traction on the thin layer of ice that had formed over the steel deck plates [4A, 4E]. "If we don't clear the upper tracking block with the bar before the next thermal drop, the condensation inside the sleeve will split the casing. We’ll be welded inside this pod until the spring melt."
Marcus Thorne stood at the base of the ladder, his heavy leather ledger case strapped flat to his ribs beneath his coat, the brass padlock resting cold against his skin [4E]. In his hands, he held a two-foot cold chisel forged from an old leaf spring salvaged from the gate heap [3D].
"The logging track is still clear down to the second lateral," Marcus said, his voice flat, rhythmic against the steady clicking of the stopwatch inside his waistcoat pocket [4E]. "But the salt tide from the delta back-surge is pooling against the five-hundred-foot line [3A, 3E]. If we don't recover the fuel pump matrix and the battery plates from the F-250 before the slush hardens into concrete, we won't have a way to draw the deep well water without burning our manual labor group to the bone [2E, 4D]."
He set the tip of the cold chisel directly against the seam where the upper locking dog met the iron casing, nodding once toward the gantry.
Elena brought the sledgehammer down—a sharp, concussive clang that filled the narrow concrete airlock with a deafening ring of steel on iron. A shower of white sparks and fractured, frozen volcanic sand exploded from the sleeve, the heavy steel door groaning as the mechanical pin shifted three millimeters inward against its frame [2E].
"Again," Marcus muttered, his fingers tightening around the cold iron of the chisel shaft, his eyes tracking the tiny fracture line opening up in the ice seal with the absolute focus of a lineman tracing a short on a live board [1A].
Outside, past the four inches of ballistic steel plate, the hills of Pine Ridge sat silent beneath a solid, monochrome indigo sky [3B, 3D]. The wind was a high-pressure katabatic roar that carried no sound from the valley floor—only the thin, dry hiss of the formaldehyde haze settling over a drowned basin where the old world’s machines had officially run out of time [3A, 3C].
