# Module 5B: The Frozen Corridor

The descent through the timber corridors of Pine Ridge did not follow the clear logging grades recorded on the United States Forest Service maps. The paths had vanished beneath six inches of a dense, hyper-dense crystalline matrix—a mixture of sub-zero ice, gray volcanic silt, and unspent atmospheric sea salt that had frozen into a slick, granite-hard crust [3A, 4A].
The ponderosa pines did not look like living timber. Their trunks were encased in a glassy layer of frozen formaldehyde haze that had dripped from the stratospheric lens, their needles locked in brittle, jade-colored clusters that shattered like glass whenever a canvas shoulder brushed the branches [3C, 5A].
Elena Vance led the six-man foraging party down the thirty-degree grade, her boots wrapped in strips of rough hemp rope to find purchase on the frozen incline [3D]. Her fingers, numbed by the sub-zero katabatic wind, were clamped tight around the steel frame of the utility handcart [4B, 4E].
"The wind is shifting off the ridge, Marc," she called back, her breath tearing away in a stiff white plume that left a rim of frost across her canvas hood [4E]. "It’s dropping below zero again. If the slush inside the F-250’s engine bay has set before we reach the canal bank, we won't be able to free the fuel pump casing without a torch [2E, 5A]."
Marcus Thorne walked three paces behind her, his hand tucked into his canvas coat to shield the leather case from the freezing mist [4E, 5A]. Every thirty paces, he stopped, pulled his grandfather’s mechanical artillery stopwatch from his pocket, and noted the elapsed interval on his split slate panel with a scrap of calcium chalk [4D].
"The time sync is holding," Marcus said, his voice flat, his teeth clicking together against the bitter cold [4D]. "We have precisely forty minutes of ambient indigo light before the tropospheric inversion drops the horizon into absolute dark [4D, 5A]. If we aren't inside the truck perimeter by then, the thermal drop will freeze the handcart wheels directly to the stone [4D]."
Behind them, Robert Cross and three other registered refugees from the lower surface shafts moved with the mechanical, unthinking gait of laborers whose caloric reserve had been halved [3D, 4D]. They carried no weapons; their hands were occupied with the heavy iron railroad pry-bars and cold chisels needed to crack the vehicle frames [3D, 5A].
They didn't look down toward the valley basin. Through the gaps in the frozen pine trunks, the San Joaquin floor was invisible, buried beneath a vast, undulating sheet of black tidewater and freezing river fog that had completely erased the suburban grid lines of Clovis and Fresno [3A, 5A]. There were no sirens, no cellular hums, no distant roar of combustion traffic—only the dry, high-altitude hiss of the sky recording the absolute cessation of the old world's connectivity [1C, 3D].
At the base of the secondary lateral, where the logging path met the gravel shoulder of the old enterprise canal system, the silhouette of the F-250 emerged from the gray gloom [2E, 3A]. It sat tilted on its ruined axles, half-buried in a smooth, frozen drift of volcanic sand that looked like a gray cement wave frozen mid-crest against the steel tailgate [3A, 5A].
