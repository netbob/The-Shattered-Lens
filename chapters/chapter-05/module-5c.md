# Module 5C: The Chassis Extraction

The hood latch of the F-250 did not release with its customary spring-loaded pop. The primary steel release cable—sheathed in a plastic conduit beneath the radiator core support—had melted into a solid, unyielding strand of black nylon resin during the short-lived thermal flash of the advanced fragment entries [1B, 2E].
Elena Vance jammed the wedge end of her five-foot railroad pry-bar directly into the seam between the crushed aluminum grille and the frozen steel lip of the hood [5B, [ITEM-001]].
"Brute force on the latch, Cross," she ordered, her voice muffled by the thick canvas backing wrapped around her face [5B]. "Throw your weight against the fulcrum before the metal slots drop into a hard freeze. If the latch pin shears inside the sleeve, we’ll have to cut the entire radiator shroud away with the cold chisels."
Robert Cross and two laborers from the secondary shafts stepped onto the ice-crusted bumper, their boots skidding across the slick volcanic sand as they threw their combined caloric mass against the iron shaft of the bar [4D, 5B].
The frozen latch casing split—a sharp, high-velocity crack of fracturing pot metal that echoed hollowly across the silent canal bed [3A, 5B]. The hood buckled upward by six inches, its underlying hinges screaming as Elena forced the steel sheet back until it folded against the cracked safety glass of the dead windshield [2E].
Inside the engine bay, the reality of the silicon death was absolute.
The truck's primary lead-acid battery plates had not merely lost their charge; the immense ionization current from the stratospheric lens wavefront had caused the thin plastic separator grids inside the casing to melt, welding the internal lead blocks into an inert, shorted mass of gray oxide [1B, 3A]. However, the auxiliary deep-cycle glass-mat battery plates sitting in the insulated secondary tray behind the firewall were physically intact—shielded from the direct EMP line by the solid iron block of the V8 engine chassis.
"Get the hand-wrenches on the terminal lugs," Marcus Thorne said, his hand checking the alignment of the ledger case against his ribs as he leaned over the fender [5A, 5B]. He didn't use an automated tool; he pulled a legacy drop-forged 1/2-inch box wrench from his coat pocket, his fingers wrapped in canvas strips to prevent his skin from welding to the sub-zero iron [5B]. "Bypass the main wiring harness entirely. The copper lines inside the loom are shorted to the frame. We only want the plates and the raw rotary vane fuel matrix."
Working in the dimming indigo light of the twilight, Elena used the cold chisel to chip away a thick layer of frozen formaldehyde slurry that encased the fuel pump assembly [3C, 5A, [ITEM-012]]. The delicate electronic control chips on the pump’s modular head were charred, their silicon gates completely blown open by the inductive surge, but the core mechanical rotary vanes and the internal brass armature windings were clean, sealed within a heavy cast-aluminum housing that had outlasted the network [1B, 2E].
"The bolts are clear," Elena muttered, her breath condensing into a heavy frost over the cold engine block. She lifted the heavy, five-pound mechanical fuel pump matrix out of the chassis, its intake lines leaking a slow, viscous stream of sub-zero diesel fuel that smelled intensely of sulfur and winterized petroleum [2E, 5B].
Cross slid the secondary battery plates and the iron pump housing into the bottom of the utility handcart, wrapping them in a protective layer of thick oilcloth to prevent the falling silica ash from fouling the intake ports [5B, [ITEM-008]]. The extraction was complete. They had salvaged the raw mechanical heart of the vehicle, leaving the dead silicon shell of the F-250 sitting on its ruined axles like an unburied iron monument to a vanished infrastructure [2E, 5B].
